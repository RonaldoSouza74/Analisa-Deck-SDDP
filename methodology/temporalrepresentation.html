Temporal Representation

Introduction

SDDP represents the process of operational decision making (generation of each plant, interconnections between regions, circuit flows, etc.) in two levels of detail. At the first level, operational decisions are periodically updated with the purpose of incorporating values observed in previous periods, such as storage levels. The new operational decisions taken in the second level are calculated as a solution of a stochastic optimization problem that seeks to balance the benefit of: (i) using more intensely the resources available in the storage devices (typically hydro plants, pump-storages, fuel storages, batteries, emission limits and some types of contracts) at that stage and thereby reducing the so-called immediate cost; and (ii) keeping them fuller and thereby reducing the so-called expected value of the future cost (the expected value comes from the uncertainty about future hydrology, wind, consumption, availability of equipment, etc.).

This first level is intrinsically related to the storage devices that create a time-coupling between stages and today's operating decisions can impact the mid and long-term operation affecting thus the future operating costs.

At the first level, it is possible to choose whether the making of strategic operative decisions is done for periods with weekly or monthly resolution (also called stages). This resolution choice depends on several factors, such as the regulation capacity of the reservoirs, the availability of historical data to adjust stochastic water inflow models and even regulatory requirements. In addition, computational effort is influenced by the study resolution (monthly or weekly), since it grows linearly with the number of stages. These decisions are made in a chronological sequential order and respect the structure of an uncertainty tree that replicates the inherent process of decision making under uncertainty.

The second level of detail refers to the making of tactical operative decisions and the calculation of the immediate cost at each stage. The least detailed (and faster) representation type would be to calculate the immediate cost for the average input data (hourly demand, wind production, hydrology, etc.), that is, aggregating the input data from all the hours of the stage into a single block or set of decisions and constraints. On the other hand, the cost of thermal generation varies in a non-linear way with the demand to be met, since the plants are dis-patched based on the increasing order of operating cost (for example, natural gas generation capacity - cheaper - is used before more expensive oil thermal plants). Due to this non-linearity, the thermal costs of supplying the average demand are lower than the resulting costs from, for example, meeting the demand represented in two blocks of hours with similar profiles (peak consumption hours and off-peak consumption hours). The most appropriate level of resolution, in terms of computational time and granularity of decisions and results, clearly depends on the individual characteristics of each system. Countries with a low level of coupling and variability between hours can have their decisions obtained quite efficiently through a block representation (also known as the "load duration curve"). On the other hand, countries that present renewables with a high level of variability and quick storage devices need a more precise representation of the hours and their chronology.

Representation of intermittent renewable generation sources and short-term characteristics

The increasing economic competitiveness of wind, solar and biomass generation sources has attracted worldwide a great deal of attention. These energy resources not only reduce green-house gas emissions, but also reduce countries' vulnerability to import fossil fuels (particularly, oil and natural gas). However, the fast penetration of these new sources has also raised some concerns for both planners and operators for two main reasons: (i) most of these sources are non-dispatchable, i.e., their generation cannot be controlled by the system operator; and (ii) their energy production presents strong volatility, i.e., the production can change significantly from one hour to the next.

To exemplify the challenges of system operators with the high penetration of intermittent renewable sources, the following figure illustrates the curves of California's net demand (demand minus intermittent renewable generation) in the past and expected in the future:

As can be seen, the penetration of intermittent renewable sources ends up causing representative impacts on the net demand profile. In addition to the change in the profile, it is worth noting the raise of the decrease/increase net demand ramps and their respective inclinations with the greater renewable penetration. These impacts lead to new operational challenges, which stand out:

  • Over-supply situations: periods when the renewable generation is higher than the demand to be met (this occurs especially in the middle of the night in regions with strong night winds);
  • Fast upward and downward ramps: dispatchable plants must have the ability to fast respond to the increase and decrease of intermittent renewable generation to maintain supply reliability and system stability;
  • Increasing thermal cycling: possible increase in the number of startups and shut-downs of thermal plants in the system due to renewable generation intermittenc

To capture these impacts accurately, a more detailed approach to the system operation at each period is essential, including a chronological and hourly representation.

The following section details the available options for representing the optimization problem solved at each stage and scenario in the SDDP (Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming) decomposition process for obtaining tactical operative decisions.

Types of intra-stage representation

The SDDP is capable of obtaining tactical operative decisions through the solution of optimization problems with different strategies and levels of resolution at each stage (week or month), divided into two groups: non-chronological and chronological representations, according to the following list:

  • Non-chronological representations:
    • Single block
    • Load duration curve (block representation)
  • Chronological representations:
    • Typical days
    • Hourly

These different types of representation will be detailed in the following sections.

Single block

In this representation, all input data, provided with block or hourly resolution, are aggregated by weighted average or sum (depending on the magnitude) and used to formulate an optimization problem with a single set of decision variables and constraints. This means that tactical operative decisions are made without considering intra-stage variations in parameters.

The procedure for creating the blocks will be illustrated below for a representation in weekly stages. In this example, the goal is to represent the demand in blocks, although the same methodology can be applied to any data with hourly resolution. This representation does not consider the fluctuation of demand and cannot reflect the high and low extremes. However, despite being less detailed, this approach provides a valid lower approximation for solving the original problem in a shorter computation time.

This formulation can be indicated for the following applications:

  • Systems with low variability and coupling: systems with these characteristics do not require the detailed representation of the input data with a set of variables and constraints for different hour blocks or even hourly.
  • Input data calibration: obtaining quick results in the initial phase of database setup or adjustments, facilitating the iterative process of verifying energy balances, validating parameters and equipment expansion schedules, and rerunning to analyze preliminary results.
  • Hybrid strategy for policy: obtaining approximate solutions in the first iterations of the policy, especially in this phase where the state variables are not yet close to the optimal solution. In the hybrid strategy, the single block representation is used in the initial iterations to generate cuts for FCF and trajectories for the state variables (with the goal of reducing computational effort), and then move to the desired temporal representation.

Load duration curve (block representation)

A widely used classic method to represent the optimization problem of the stage, while avoiding the explicit representation of constraints and decision variables for each hour, is through the grouping of different operational conditions throughout each stage (weekly or monthly), aggregating the hours into up to 24 groups, "clusters," or blocks of hours. The block representation allows for an approximate representation of the problem's non-linearities. The curve obtained by aggregating the operational conditions into non-chronological hour blocks is also known as the load duration curve.

The next step is to sort the 168 hours demand values in descending order as shown in the following figure.

The non-chronological demand curve in descending order is also known as the Load Duration Curve. This ordering facilitates the aggregation of similar consumption levels in blocks, as the following figure illustrates by aggregating in 3 blocks.

It is observed that the representation with 3 blocks, unlike the single block, can better capture the fluctuation of demand, getting closer to the high and low extremes. Moreover, it is possible to group blocks in such a way that we have maximum and minimum values represented by smaller blocks, but with values closer to reality.

However, when comparing with the load duration curve with 10 blocks in the following figure, there is a significant improvement in the approximation, although this requires a greater computational effort to solve the optimization problem.

In general, the load block curve adjustment procedure aims to minimize the sum of the differences (absolute or quadratic) between the average demand of each block and hourly demands. Additionally, it is important to preserve the total energy amount in the stage. In the example presented above, this means that average demand (in MW) of each block times the respective duration (in hours) must be equal to the sum (in MWh) of the 168 hourly demands. This procedure for creating blocks can be applied to any data with hourly resolution.

In more complex cases, the blocks are usually constructed based on multivariate cluster analysis algorithms such as the k-means. For instance, this approach might be important in cases with two countries with different demand patterns or systems with strong renewable penetration.

It is straightforward to observe that the larger the number of blocks, the better the load curve will be represented. The choice of the number of blocks to be used depends basically on the commitment relationship between precision and computational effort (which increases non-linearly with the number of blocks).

Typical days representation

Motivation

The planning of the expansion and operation of energy systems is challenging because it requires proper modeling of relevant elements such as power plants, transmission lines, slow and fast response storages (hydro reservoirs, natural gas and batteries). It is also necessary to represent the underlying uncertainties affecting the decision-making process (hydro inflows, failures, renewable sources, fuel prices and buy/sell, etc.), the correct horizon (which varies from months to decades), and the appropriate time scale. Due to its complexity and the extensive computational time needed to represent the hourly scale, it is common to approximate the temporal resolution within each stage (week or month) using a load duration curve approach for the planning phases of expansion and operation, as seen in the previous sections.

However, the high volatility of the constantly growing share of renewable generation, such as wind and solar plants, poses new challenges to medium and long-term expansion and operation planning models because it requires modeling the system's dynamics at an hourly level to assess the energy systems' flexibility needs.

In this context, it is necessary to represent variables (generation of plants, storage levels, etc.) and constraints (load supply, transition equations, etc.) for each hour. Additionally, other operational constraints, such as unit commitment and ramp limits, can also play a significant role. However, simply extending the model to hourly representation can significantly increase the problem's dimension, leading to computational intractability for medium and large systems. To reduce computational effort, the representation by typical days within each stage presents itself as an intermediate strategy between block representation and hourly representation, aiming to capture important operational characteristics without extensive solution times.

This modeling represents each hour of each typical day in each stage, thus respecting the chronology within each day. Therefore, it is suitable for medium and long-term study horizons, efficiently incorporating important short-term constraints into the decision-making process.

The main available features are:

  • Reservoir management with hourly resolution.
  • Small storages such as batteries and run-of-rivers with hourly granularity per typical day.
  • Hydroelectric and thermal operating constraints, including:
    • Generation ramp-up and ramp-down, outflow ramp-up and ramp-down for hydro plants
    • Generation ramp-up and ramp-down, minimum downtime, minimum uptime, maximum uptime and shutdown cost for thermal plants
  • Detailed outputs with hourly resolution (per typical day).

Overview of the methodology

The typical days methodology allows for an intermediate representation between block and hourly representations, as it preserves the hourly chronology within each typical day. This enables a detailed representation of data fluctuations and their extreme values, in addition to the short-term characteristics involving equipment with faster dynamics than those used for strategic operative decisions. In this way, a compromise solution is expected to be found that represents the dynamics of strategic and tactical decisions with optimized computational time.

Considering the demand data for a period of one week, it is observed that the hourly data have seven "cycles," corresponding to the days of the week. It is also noted that the first two cycles are different (and with lower demand) than the others, as they correspond to Saturday and Sunday (if there were a holiday in the middle of the week, it would have a daily profile similar to Sunday's).

Important observations

In the optimization problem, the SDDP will represent the complete set of variables and constraints for 24 hours of each typical day, along with the detailed commitment of units, generation reserves (including batteries and other storage devices) and the transport model (interconnections or the complete transmission network model also available). For example, a case with 5 blocks defined to represent two typical days will have 2x24=48 sets of variables and constraints, representing approximately a 10-fold increase in the size of the optimization problem. The solution to an optimization problem is combinatorial in nature, which can result in longer execution times than the proportionate increase in the number of variables and con-straints relative to the original size.

Note

Although this representation has been widely tested in various cases, we rely on the experiences of our users to continuously improve both the methodology and the efficiency of the algorithm. Please contact us if you have any suggestions or encounter difficulties.

Hourly representation

When the hourly representation is activated, the optimization problems solved will explicitly be defined with hourly resolution. This means that the number of variables and constraints are proportional to the number of hours at each stage. This representation can significantly increase the computational effort required to solve the optimization problems with hourly granularity when compared to the block representation at each stage Additionally, the volume of output results is also significantly larger. Therefore, a careful analysis of the user's need of increasing granularity is suggested, which should mainly be based on the objectives of the executions and the effects that are desired to be captured.

Note

Solving these hourly problems efficiently was a challenging task that required the development of new algorithms and procedures to ensure the solution quality while keeping the computational times acceptable. Even so, if any problems or high execution times associated to the hourly representation occur the user is recommended to contact PSR at sddp@psr-inc.com immediately.

First Steps

Data definition

In theory, it would be possible to allow all input data to be informed in hourly resolution for the entire study horizon. However, since SDDP is a mid and long-term dispatch tool, many of these data have a low frequency of variation and their representation on a weekly or monthly basis is suitable. Even in the case of a higher variation frequency it is possible to identify standardized variation profiles over a stage. Therefore, an hourly representation for all data, besides being unnecessary, would require a large volume of input data that might not be available.

In this way, a flexible approach was adopted so that only a few additional input data is required to obtain results with hourly representation. Through the hour-block mapping table described below, it is possible to recreate the chronology of each stage by associating the values of the corresponding block to each hour. This approach is very flexible because it allows an existing

SDDP database to be easily used as a starting point for obtaining results with hourly detailing. Besides, it's worth to highlight that it is possible to refine the model to the most relevant quantities by adding hourly input data (such as hourly demand, intermittent renewable generation scenarios, etc.), which will be used instead of data mapped from data defined by blocks.

In this context, data per block are treated as different data configurations (or clusters) within each stage. In other words, up to 21 different data sets can be defined for each stage and this data will be used to automatically recreate the chronological hourly representation within each stage, once an hour-block mapping is defined.

  • Duration of each block (in hours)

Calculation of the duration of the blocks from the hour-block mapping table

The hour-block mapping table also implicitly defines the duration of the blocks in each week/month. The duration of each block is calculated by counting the number of hours associated with each block. Therefore, when the hour-block mapping data is used, the original data of fixed duration and variable duration of the blocks will be disregarded.

Input Data

The resolution of the input data defined is independent of the resolution selected for the study execution, by block or hourly. In other words, the user has the flexibility to define the data as desired and the model can transform the input data informed according to the resolution of the study:

  • For studies that consider block representation:
    • Data defined per block: are directly used to build the optimization problem with block resolution;
    • Hourly data: are automatically aggregated into blocks using the user-defined hour-block mapping. Finally, the data aggregated in blocks are used to build the optimization problem with block resolution. For this reason, if the user wants to use hourly data, it is also necessary to inform the hour-block mapping data.
  • For studies that consider hourly representation:
    • Data defined per block: are automatically mapped in the hours using the hour-block mapping to obtain hourly data from the block data. Finally, these hourly chronological data are used to build the optimization problem with hourly resolution;
    • Hourly data: are directly used in the assembly of the optimization problem with hourly resolution;

Additionally, it is worth noting that data only need to be informed for the selected resolution. For example, if the user chooses to inform hourly demand data, the demand by block data definition is not needed. The same applies to the renewable generation scenarios.

Note

As previously highlighted, some input data will still be informed with block resolution and, due to this fact, the hour-block mapping must be informed for all studies that consider hourly representation.

Input data resolution

Before executing the model, the user must select the desired resolution for each data type. This selection must be defined in the Data resolution screen.

Hour-block mapping data

The primary purpose of the hour-block mapping data is to establish the relationship between the hours and blocks in each stage. Thus, these data are optional in the case of studies with block representation and mandatory in case studies with hourly representation.

Although studies with block resolution do not represent hours individually in the optimization problem formulation, the hour-block mapping data can be used to obtain the duration of the blocks (instead of using the fixed duration or the variable duration of the blocks).

On the other side, in studies with hourly resolution, the mapping data is used to reconstruct the hourly chronology of the data that are informed by block.

Integrated clustering tools

There are two tools integrated into SDDP related to the hour-block mapping:

  1. Block representation: This feature helps from scratch or replaces the hour-block mapping for the selected number of blocks, if there already exists an hour-block mapping defined in the database. It is accessed in Clustering: Hour-block mapping.

  2. Hour-block remap: This feature helps create a new hour-block mapping for a different (desired) number of blocks. For example, it is possible to have data defined for 5 blocks and run the model for 15 blocks, which can be chronological or not. It is enabled by simply activating the Use a different configuration of blocks option in Horizon and resolution. SDDP will run the case with the desired (different) number of blocks without any additional information. The remapping tool is accessed in Clustering: Hour-block remap.

Both options take the hourly demand data already defined in the SDDP database as input data and use a multivariate cluster analysis algorithm called k-means to create the blocks. The objective is to minimize approximation errors for the user-defined number of blocks while assigning (clustering) the hours into the blocks, as in the example shown below:

It is worth remembering that the hour-block mapping must be unique for the entire database. Now, imagine that you have two systems defined in the database and system 1 has a demand much bigger than the other one. If the user does not activate the Normalize data option, the demand of system 1 will be much more representative while calculating the hour-block mapping than the demand of system 2. In other words, the size of each demand will reflect its representativeness on the "total" demand that will be used to assess the hour-block mapping. On the other hand, if you activate the Normalize data option, the demands of systems 1 and 2 will be first normalized in a separate way and after that they will be used to calculate the hour-block mapping. As a consequence, both demands will have the same representativeness on the hour-block mapping assessment.

Additionally, if the user does not activate the Peak and/or Off-peak options, all blocks will be automatically calculated by the algorithm. On the other hand, if the user activates the Peak option and sets the Number of blocks equal to 1, for example, the Peak block won't be calculated, it will be arbitrarily defined by the user. In this case, all values above the threshold will be in block 1. So, if the Thresholdg is equal to 0.02 p.u., the model will take the 2% highest demand values and assign them to the peak load block. If the user sets the "Number of blocks" equal to 2, then, the first two blocks will be arbitrarily defined by the user based on the threshold value.

The same behavior is valid for the Off-peak option, i.e., if the user activates the Off-peak option, sets the Number of blocks equal to 1 and the Threshold equal to 0.02 p.u., the model will take the 2% lowest demand values and assign them to the off-peak load block.

Hourly demand data

Optionally, the demand data can be informed in hourly resolution. These data are provided through the graphical interface in Demand, in the Hourly tables. Data can also be imported/exported to Excel via CSV (comma-separated) files.

Hourly scenarios for the generation of renewable sources

Optionally, scenarios for the generation of renewable sources can be informed on an hourly basis. These data should be informed through the interface in Renewable Station in the Hourly tables. Data can also be imported/exported to Excel via CSV (comma-separated) files.

Operating constraints of thermal plants

One of the main motivations for performing hourly executions is to capture chronological effects not seen in executions with block representation. In this case, users will have the option of representing thermal operational chronological constraints, such as: ramp-up/down, minimum up/downtime, maximum number of start-ups/shutdowns and so on.

These data can be informed through the graphical interface in the Thermal plant's Operating constraints. Data can also be imported/exported to Excel via CSV (comma-separated) files.

Operating constraints of hydro plants

Users will also have the option of representing hydro operational chronological constraints, such as: water travel time for turbining/spilling, outflow ramp-up/down and ramp-up/down (in MW/min).

These data can be informed through the graphical interface in the Hydro plant's Operating constraints. Data can also be imported/exported to Excel via CSV (comma-separated) files.

Study options

The default option for the economic dispatch is the aggregated representation by blocks, where the operational decisions within each stage (week or month) are made taking into consideration a representation of up to 21 blocks of hours.

To activate the chronological hourly representation, after defining the hour-block mapping and choosing the data resolution to be used as previously explained, the option Hourly representation must be activated in the Horizon and resolution tab in the Configuration screen.

After the completion of each hourly execution, it is very important to check the heatmap called Execution status per stage and scenario available in the SDDP Dashboard.

Heatmap of the execution status per stage and scenario

Some of the underlying MIP optimization problems solved by SDDP may require substantial computational effort, especially those related to the hourly representation. To get a better overview of the trade-off between solution quality x computational effort, there is a heatmap called "Execution Status per stage and scenario" in the SDDP Dashboard. In this graphic, the status of the problem solved in each stage and scenario is illustrated in a heatmap, according to the following color scheme:

Color Meaning Description
🟢 Success The mixed integer programming problem (MIP) was successfully solved, finding an optimal solution within the defined convergence tolerance.
🔵 Feasible solution The maximum CPU time was reached, and the feasible solution found exceeds the MIP convergence tolerance.
🟡 Warning The maximum CPU time set for the search for the MIP was reached, and no feasible solution was found; however, a linearized solution was found for the MIP linear relaxation. In this linearized solution, the binary variables associated with unit commitment decisions and other binary variables considered in the problem can take values between 0 and 1, and not strictly 0 or 1, which means that the solution may not meet all the constraints considered in the problem.
🔴 Error No feasible solution (integer or linearized) was found within the maximum CPU time.

It is very important to review this heatmap after the hourly runs.
